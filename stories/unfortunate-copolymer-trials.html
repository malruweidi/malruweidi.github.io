A notebook named after the experiment

A Series of Unfortunate Trials.

My friends and I were working on different copolymers. Each of us had a material of our own to investigate, so even when we were sharing a laboratory and comparing notes, we were not quite performing the same experiment. Mine developed an especially stubborn problem: the polymer appeared to have become crosslinked, and it would not dissolve the way I had expected. I repeated the attempts, changed what I could, and watched the same basic difficulty return. Eventually I gave the notebook a title: A Series of Unfortunate Trials.

There is a useful piece of chemistry behind the frustration. A conventional, soluble diblock copolymer has two chemically distinct polymer segments joined along a chain. Depending on their composition and solvent environment, such chains may dissolve or assemble into micelles, with one segment preferring the surrounding liquid and the other avoiding it. A covalently crosslinked material is different. When chains become joined into a network, the resulting polymer can resist dissolution even in a solvent that would dissolve the uncrosslinked chains. It may swell, but the network does not simply separate into individual molecules.

That was how I understood what had happened to my material. But I do not want to turn a recollection into a structural determination. Insolubility alone cannot distinguish unwanted covalent crosslinking from inadequate solvent conditions, aggregation, crystallinity or other changes in the material. Confirming a network would have required evidence beyond a failed dissolution attempt, such as systematic solvent extraction and swelling measurements together with appropriate chemical characterization. I can document the observation and my interpretation; the photograph of the notebook cannot establish the mechanism.

What I remember most clearly is the contrast between my work and that of my friends. We had been assigned different copolymer systems, and that made the conversations about our results particularly vivid. Chemistry was no longer the tidy movement from a reaction scheme to a product. Two materials belonging to the same broad family could behave differently for reasons hidden in their molecular architecture or preparation. The experiment that would not cooperate demanded more thinking, not less.

The title was partly a joke at my own expense, and partly an accurate laboratory record. I had no triumphant result to put on the cover, so I named it after the experience of trying. Years later, I find that more revealing than a photograph of a successful flask. It captures a period when I was learning that an inconvenient observation is still an observation, and that a scientist's responsibility begins before there is a satisfying explanation for it.

What the sources establish

The assignment of different copolymers to friends, the difficulty dissolving my material and the interpretation of possible crosslinking are my firsthand recollections. The 8 October 2017 date is the archived post date, not a verified date for each attempt. Separately, the QNRF project UREP19-110-1-014 investigated polymeric micelles for removing polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons from environmental samples, using commercially obtained block copolymers and Flory–Huggins compatibility calculations. Its final report does not document this synthesis setback, so I do not present the notebook as a result from that grant.
