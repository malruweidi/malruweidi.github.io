RUWAIDIX / PERSONAL SCIENTIFIC HISTORIES

The stories between the experiments.

A paper records the question, the methods and what survived scrutiny. It rarely explains why a particular question mattered to the person asking it.

These are accounts from my life in science, told alongside the photographs and research records that can support them. A memory is not an experimental result, and I intend to keep the distinction visible.

012011 / SCHOOL

Origin of a question

Anatomy was not an illustration anymore.

I remember the practical lesson more clearly than I remember many examinations. There was a fox specimen before us, not a tidy diagram in a textbook. For the first time, the arrangement of organs was something I could examine directly. A body was not a collection of labels. Its structures occupied space, touched other structures and depended on one another.

I would be giving the experience too much importance if I claimed it predetermined my career. At the time, I was a school student trying to understand what was in front of me. Its lasting effect was more modest and perhaps more useful: it made me dissatisfied with explanations that stop at the boundary of a single part. Many years later, when I moved between chemistry, cell biology and engineering, I recognized the same difficulty in a different form.

My own experience of illness began around that period, but that is a separate history. I do not treat one as the explanation for the other. The anatomy room belongs here because it gave me an early encounter with direct observation and with the limits of a simplified model.

022015–2019 / COMMUNITY

Learning to make science public

A chemistry society was another kind of laboratory.

University taught me techniques and equations, but much of my education took place in the hours when other students were still willing to discuss a problem after a class had ended. Helping establish the American Chemical Society student chapter at Qatar University in 2015 was part of that experience. We wanted experiments, talks and scientific conversations to reach beyond the people already working in research groups.

The chemistry festivals brought this into focus. When I hosted the fifth Chemistry Festival, the work was less about displaying what I knew than about making room for other people to become interested. Public science requires a different sort of precision. One has to be understandable without pretending that an experiment is simpler, safer or more conclusive than it is.

I still regard student scientific communities as part of research training, not a ceremonial distraction from it. The appreciation certificate I received for helping establish the chapter matters to me chiefly because it records work that was shared with colleagues, students and mentors.

032016–2021 / EXPERIMENTS

From molecules to physiology

The living system refused to behave like a flask.

Chemical synthesis had offered a pleasing degree of control. I could change a structure, adjust a condition and look for the consequence. Research on zebrafish cranial vessels and primary cilia complicated that picture. Blood flow, cell behaviour and development were not separate questions merely because we could measure them separately.

Experiments on the injury experienced by airway epithelial cells during airway reopening brought me to another scale. A force applied to a fluid could become a biological event at a cell surface. In a manuscript, the mechanics and the cellular response need to be defined and examined carefully. In the laboratory, the lesson also had a personal dimension: making a convincing model did not mean that the biology had agreed to follow it.

The photographs and recordings are useful precisely because they are not decorative proof of a conclusion. They show apparatus, specimens and observations. The corresponding figures, methods and papers are where evidence for a scientific claim must be sought.

There is also a Ramadan memory behind this microscopy. On the final experimental day, people were waiting at my house while I was still working. Earlier evenings had meant iftar at university followed by experiments and missed Taraweeh prayers.

04RAMADAN / ZEBRAFISH VASCULAR BIOLOGY

An evening that did not end at iftar

The guests were waiting. I was still at the microscope.

On the last day of that experiment, people had already gathered inside my house. They were waiting for me, and I had not arrived. I was still at Qatar University, occupied with the work that had taken over so much of that Ramadan. I remember the contrast more clearly than I remember the hour: one room prepared for a gathering, another room where the experiment was not yet finished.

The days before had acquired a routine of their own. We would break our fast at the university, then go back to the experiments. On those evenings we missed Taraweeh prayers because we were still working. That detail matters to the memory. Ramadan is not an ordinary month with a meal shifted to sunset; its evenings have a rhythm of prayer, companionship and obligations to people. We were making choices about that time, not moving through an empty calendar.

The work I associate with those nights concerned endothelial primary cilia in the developing blood vessels of zebrafish. These are tiny cellular projections, and the questions surrounding them were anything but tiny. Where do they appear as the brain's vascular network forms? How is their distribution related to development and blood flow? What can their loss tell us about the stability of a vessel? In microscopy, the object of a question can occupy scarcely any space in the image while requiring enormous care in interpretation.

A microscope does not know that people are waiting for you. It does not recognize the end of a working day or the beginning of Taraweeh. And biology does not become more legible because one is tired or has promised to be somewhere else. The experiment has to be observed as it is, not as the timetable would prefer it to be. None of this makes staying late automatically wise. It explains why leaving can become a much harder decision than a neat research biography admits.

I do not want to turn missed prayers or waiting guests into decorations on a story about professional dedication. They were real things missed and real people kept waiting. Nor do I claim that the last night produced a singular discovery. I remember it because the work and the rest of life were competing for the same hours, and I could not give those hours to both.

Our collaborative study appeared in 2018 in Arteriosclerosis, Thrombosis, and Vascular Biology (ATVB), examining the distribution and function of endothelial cilia during zebrafish cerebral-vascular development. A scientific paper properly records the evidence that its authors can defend. It cannot record every iftar at the university or explain to the guests in my house why I was late. That is part of what an archive like this can preserve, without confusing the story of an evening with proof of a scientific conclusion.

052022–2026 / ONGOING

Research not yet finished

A formulation is a promise to face physiology.

By the time I entered graduate pharmaceutical sciences in 2022, I had become interested in the distance between what a molecule can do in a controlled experiment and what it might accomplish at a site of treatment. Drug delivery is where that distance becomes difficult to ignore. A therapeutic molecule must survive the route by which we propose to administer it.

My current MSc work studies inhalable dexamethasone niosomes and proniosomes. Nebulization, aerosol behaviour and vesicle characteristics are not secondary engineering details. They determine whether a formulation's proposed advantages remain meaningful once it has to travel. After resuming graduate study in 2025, I returned to that problem with questions shaped by my earlier work on the lung.

The project remains in progress. I do not want the website to transform unfinished measurements into reported results. This chapter records the motivation and the research direction, with the thesis and experimental evidence to speak for themselves when they are ready.

OTHER SCENES / STORIES THAT DON'T FIT A STRAIGHT LINE

There were other beginnings.

The opening five chapters follow the main arc of the research and the experiences around it. These remaining episodes move between earlier years and different disciplines. They belong here because a life in science is not a tidy chronology: some of its most consequential lessons occur to the side of the planned experiment.

06ABOUT 2014 / MOLECULES

Early chemical thinking

The model in my hands was not the molecule.

In a photograph from when I was about nineteen, I am holding a ball-and-stick model of a six-membered ring. I was fascinated by the power of such an object: a few spheres and rods could make an invisible arrangement seem almost tangible. Yet it would be careless to call the model benzene or cyclohexane from the photograph alone. Its visible bonds do not settle the chemical identity. Even an apparently simple picture has limits.

That became a useful lesson during my training in synthetic chemistry and chemical sensing. An imine can be drawn perfectly on a page; the experimental question is whether it forms as proposed, survives the conditions, and produces a response distinguishable from other explanations. My undergraduate research on imines as chemical sensors belonged to that discipline of asking a structure to justify itself through measurements. A photograph preserves curiosity; an analytical figure requires controls, methods and the possibility of being wrong.

People before publications

Not every early lesson took place in a laboratory.

I was a young student, not a medical expert. I do not treat these photographs as evidence of a clinical contribution; they are evidence of participation, of encounters with people, and of the beginning of a responsibility that I would later carry into scientific outreach and teaching.

082017 / CAIRO TO QATAR

A microscope of my own

The microscope I brought home from Cairo.

I bought this microscope in the Qasr El Einy area of Cairo, a city closely tied to my roots, and brought it back to Qatar. That sounds like a purchase followed by a journey. What the sentence leaves out is the hassle of getting it there. I wanted the instrument badly enough to put up with the trouble, because I wanted to be able to look at things whenever a question occurred to me.

In the photograph, the microscope is standing in my room, among books, notes and an American Chemical Society mug. It was a vacation morning, not an assigned laboratory session. There was no experiment to finish for a supervisor and no result to present. I liked having the possibility of observing something simply because it interested me, and of spending as long as I wanted trying to understand what I saw.

That sort of freedom mattered to my education. At university, instruments came with protocols, schedules and good reasons for both. At home, the microscope gave my curiosity somewhere to go between those structured encounters. I could follow my own interests, return to an observation and learn to notice the difference between seeing something and knowing what it meant.

Of course, buying a microscope did not make me a scientist, any more than owning a camera makes someone a photographer. Proper research still requires controls, reproducible methods and explanations that survive criticism. But that instrument helped me develop the habit that made me want to become one. When I look at the photograph now, I see neither a laboratory result nor a decorative still life. I see a piece of Cairo that travelled with me to Qatar, and an ordinary morning on which science belonged to my own time.

092017 / POLYMER CHEMISTRY

A notebook named after the experiment

A Series of Unfortunate Trials.

My friends and I were working on different copolymers. Each of us had a material of our own to investigate, so even when we were sharing a laboratory and comparing notes, we were not quite performing the same experiment. Mine developed an especially stubborn problem: the polymer appeared to have become crosslinked, and it would not dissolve the way I had expected. I repeated the attempts, changed what I could, and watched the same basic difficulty return. Eventually I gave the notebook a title: A Series of Unfortunate Trials.

There is a useful piece of chemistry behind the frustration. A conventional, soluble diblock copolymer has two chemically distinct polymer segments joined along a chain. Depending on their composition and solvent environment, such chains may dissolve or assemble into micelles, with one segment preferring the surrounding liquid and the other avoiding it. A covalently crosslinked material is different. When chains become joined into a network, the resulting polymer can resist dissolution even in a solvent that would dissolve the uncrosslinked chains. It may swell, but the network does not simply separate into individual molecules.

That was how I understood what had happened to my material. But I do not want to turn a recollection into a structural determination. Insolubility alone cannot distinguish unwanted covalent crosslinking from inadequate solvent conditions, aggregation, crystallinity or other changes in the material. Confirming a network would have required evidence beyond a failed dissolution attempt, such as systematic solvent extraction and swelling measurements together with appropriate chemical characterization. I can document the observation and my interpretation; the photograph of the notebook cannot establish the mechanism.

What I remember most clearly is the contrast between my work and that of my friends. We had been assigned different copolymer systems, and that made the conversations about our results particularly vivid. Chemistry was no longer the tidy movement from a reaction scheme to a product. Two materials belonging to the same broad family could behave differently for reasons hidden in their molecular architecture or preparation. The experiment that would not cooperate demanded more thinking, not less.

The title was partly a joke at my own expense, and partly an accurate laboratory record. I had no triumphant result to put on the cover, so I named it after the experience of trying. Years later, I find that more revealing than a photograph of a successful flask. It captures a period when I was learning that an inconvenient observation is still an observation, and that a scientist's responsibility begins before there is a satisfying explanation for it.

102017–2019 / HISTORY

When science becomes a historical question

The periodic table has a history, not just a shape.

Studying history alongside chemistry changed the way I looked at scientific knowledge. A textbook can arrange the periodic table so neatly that it seems to have appeared complete. The historian has to ask what was known at the time, which alternatives were plausible, and how a particular classification won its authority. That is not a criticism of chemistry. It is a way of respecting how difficult chemistry was to build.

A university visit involving Eric Scerri, historian and philosopher of chemistry, belonged to one side of that curiosity. An ACS student chapter astrochemistry event with Professor Yousef Hijji belonged to another. The subjects differ, but both resist the notion that a discipline is a fixed shelf of established facts. Scientific ideas have histories, and their possible applications reach into settings far beyond the laboratory we happen to know.

112020–2024 / BIOMATERIALS

The importance of interfaces

Sometimes the surface is the experiment.

My work across polymeric materials, electrospinning and plasma surface modification made one distinction increasingly important: the bulk chemistry of a material is not necessarily the chemistry that the cell, the liquid or the surrounding environment encounters. Fibres change geometry and accessible area. Plasma processing can change the outermost surface chemistry. Neither technique can be understood from its name alone; preparation conditions and characterization decide what was actually produced.

The archived scanning-electron microscopy figure of PLA morphology shown below is part of that research world. It invites close looking but not conclusions unsupported by its original controls. I see a continuity between this work and drug delivery, although a modified polymer surface is not an inhaled vesicle. In both, the material eventually has to meet something more complicated than the assumptions under which it was designed.

122016–2026 / APPARATUS

Reading what a photograph proves

A machine is not an experiment.

I have worked in laboratories where nearly every surface seemed to hold a different question: a pipette at the molecular bench, a stereomicroscope beside positioning hardware, a biological safety cabinet ready for cell work. Later, cardiovascular imaging brought me into another world of instrumentation. These settings all reward technical care, but possession of an instrument does not establish mastery of it, and its photograph does not tell us which protocol was running that day.

That is why I prefer to preserve a photograph with a careful caption over turning it into a heroic scene. The image of a spectral Doppler waveform documents the presence of a clinical imaging display, not a diagnosis. A short clip of a pump-like assembly documents apparatus in motion, not its exact model or function. If an instrument is to support a scientific claim, the acquisition conditions, calibration, analysis and surrounding record must do the heavier work.

These ordinary laboratory images might be among the most revealing things in an archive. They remind me that a scientific result rests on hours of preparation that seldom survive the final manuscript. The apparatus is present in the story, but the evidence still has to be earned.
