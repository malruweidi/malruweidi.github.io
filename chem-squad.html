01 / The beginning

Before the publications,
there was a laboratory.

In 2016, we found each other through organic chemistry. The laboratory gave us methods to learn, unfamiliar materials to investigate and reasons to stay curious long after the assigned work was finished. We were particularly interested in the extraction of botanical oils and constituents from materials such as myrrh and nutmeg, and in examining what their chemical composition might mean for possible medicinal uses.

Those questions required more than enthusiasm. Extracting something is not the same as identifying its active constituents, and traditional use is not proof of pharmacological benefit. Part of what made the conversations enjoyable was learning how chemistry, analytical evidence and biological testing could connect a promising idea to a defensible scientific conclusion.

What endured was not a single experiment or research topic. It was the habit of returning to the literature, imagining what could be tested, and calling one another when a better question appeared.

We did not keep meeting because a supervisor assigned another project. We kept meeting because we wanted to understand more.

03 / A standing conversation

Once a week,
science has a seat.

The weekly gatherings are not conferences, compulsory seminars or a commercial programme. They are a place where scientists can talk without needing a deliverable before the conversation is worth having. A paper, a synthetic route, a strange result, or a speculative hypothesis can all be reasons to bring everyone together.

Independent thought benefits from company. The questions become sharper when someone else is willing to disagree, suggest an experiment, or expose an assumption that seemed obvious.

01

Bring a question.

Anything from reaction chemistry and natural products to a biomedical or pharmaceutical puzzle.

02

Follow the evidence.

Talk through mechanisms, methods, contradictory findings and the literature behind a claim.

03

Think beyond your own field.

One person's chemistry problem may connect to another's interest in biology, materials or therapeutic design.

04

Keep the conversation alive.

Not every discussion needs to become a publication. Sometimes the reward is a better understanding.

04 / From one subject to many

Our interests have
grown with us.

Our common training is chemistry. Across our individual work, independent collaborations and discussions, that foundation has led us toward several kinds of research questions. The areas below describe the intellectual range of the circle, not a claim that all seven members jointly conducted every project.

01 / Roots

Organic chemistry & natural products

Extraction, chemical identity, molecular transformation and the evaluation of proposed biological activity.

02 / Molecules

Medicinal & pharmaceutical chemistry

Structure–activity reasoning, therapeutic hypotheses, drug properties and design questions.

03 / Materials

Formulations & biomaterials

Carrier systems, polymer-based technologies and the relationship between material properties and performance.

04 / Evidence

Research papers & scientific writing

Literature evaluation, collaboration across projects and turning methods and ideas into carefully argued scholarship.

05 / Methods

Quantitative & computational thinking

How data analysis, modelling and experimental design can improve the interpretation of chemical and biomedical questions.

06 / People

Education & scientific exchange

Sharing knowledge, mentoring, communicating difficult ideas and making science a continuing conversation.

05 / Where the paths led

Separate careers.
A shared beginning.

From the same organic chemistry laboratory, we eventually followed different academic and professional paths. Five of the seven pursued master's-level study: four in Qatar and Ahmed Elgendy in Canada. Our interests expanded into fields including environmental science and solar-cell research, while education became another direction represented in the circle. The original foundation in chemistry remained, but the questions each person brought back grew more varied.

That variety has changed our conversations for the better. Different research problems demand different kinds of evidence, and an idea that seems settled within one discipline can look quite different from another. We have continued meeting and collaborating independently, not because our careers followed the same route, but because each route gives us something new to learn from one another.

Some members of the circle are also among the founding members of Majales Education. That connection reflects how a long friendship in science can extend into educational entrepreneurship, while Chem Squad remains its own informal community.

What ten years taught us

A scientific community can begin with nothing more than people who care about the questions.

There is value in institutions, laboratories and funding. There is also value in the discussions that happen between them: where a paper is challenged, an experiment is imagined and somebody says, “Have you thought about this another way?”

Chem Squad has stayed close to that original impulse. The careers develop. The subjects change. The reason to meet remains.

An important distinction

Independent in spirit.
Rigorous in method.

Chem Squad is an informal peer circle, not a registered laboratory, institutional research centre, degree programme or clinical testing service. Research contributions and publications are attributed to their actual authors and institutions. Exploring natural products or discussing potential medicinal uses does not establish safety, efficacy or clinical benefit.

The group's history and member list here are recorded from its members' account. The original laboratory interests are documented as beginnings, not presented as proven medical discoveries.
