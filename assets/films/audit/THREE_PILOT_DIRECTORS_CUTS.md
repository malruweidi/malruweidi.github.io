## 01 · TWO MATERIALS. NO SECTION.
**Target:** 02:30. **Mode:** tactile laboratory investigation. **Voice:** first-person, sparse and unemphatic. **Sound:** a quiet room tone or licensed restrained constructed texture, never passed off as the actual 2017 microtome recording.

### New complete narration

Look at the block, not the blade. It is mounted in the microtome, but what comes away from it is not the ribbon I needed. There should have been usable sections of a zebrafish specimen, thin enough to examine the anatomy without losing the orientation. There weren't any.

The difficult part started earlier, when the tiny specimens had to be held at precisely the right angle. I used a two-percent agarose support before the next stages of processing. It seemed a practical way of keeping that orientation. Paraffin would then supply the material support needed for cutting.

Each stage made sense in isolation. At the blade they no longer behaved as a single block. The layers separated. The material broke apart. I had prepared something that a microtome could encounter, but not a preparation it could section as intended.

There is a plausible explanation in the materials. Agarose is hydrophilic; paraffin is not. Under those conditions, the boundary between them may not have formed the mechanically coherent interface that sectioning demanded. That explanation fits what I saw. I did not measure the interfacial strength or prove that it was the only cause.

I could have changed the embedding approach and tried again with a different preparation. This particular attempt never produced an interpretable histological section. The photograph at the microtome is the last useful record of what happened.

## 02 · MAKING MEDICINES KEEP
**Target:** 02:30. **Mode:** historical material investigation. **Voice:** a curious historian-pharmaceutical scientist, not a celebration of medieval achievement. **Sound:** close material sound only from an identified contemporary illustrative experiment, with sparse music.

### New complete narration

A recipe can name the ingredients. It cannot, by itself, settle what happens after they have been heated, strained, stored, and used. Sugar may crystallize. A liquid can change. The maker must still decide whether the preparation is fit for its intended purpose.

In the thirteenth-century pharmacy manual *Minhāj al-dukkān*, the chapter on syrups starts with *julāb*, a preparation based on sugar and rose water. The manual then treats the process as work that requires judgment: heating, removing foam, straining, and recognizing *qiwām*, the consistency the maker was trying to achieve. These are practical instructions. They are not modern instrument readings.

A document among the Cairo Genizah manuscripts gives a different glimpse of practice. It orders powdered ingredients to be kneaded with rose syrup. The prescription is evidence of what was written, not a photograph of a compound that was certainly dispensed.

There was another difficulty: what could the pharmacist do when an ingredient could not be obtained? A substitution had to be argued from the preparation's intended function and the circumstances. It was not permission to sell a deceptive mixture under the old name.

This is the question I pursued in *Making Medicines Keep*: how a preparation, made from variable materials under imperfect conditions, could be judged dependable enough to leave the shop. The paper is an inquiry into the historical craft and its safeguards. It is not a claim that medieval apothecaries invented today's drug-quality regulations.

## 03 · THE LABORATORY WE CARRIED WITH US
**Target:** 02:15. **Mode:** a warm scientific-archive essay; the people are the subject. **Voice:** conversational or author-approved original narration, with space for actual colleague recording **only when permission/context is verified**.

### New complete narration

One of the older photographs shows a chemistry room before any of us knew where the work would take us. There is equipment on the bench, the beginnings of an extraction, and the ordinary expectation that the afternoon might produce something worth discussing.

Chem Squad grew from those conversations around organic chemistry in 2016. We were not a research institute. We were seven chemistry graduates who kept finding reasons to read, argue, and return to questions left unfinished by a class. An extract could be made in an afternoon. Understanding what it contained was a different undertaking, and proving a medicinal effect would demand far more.

Our American Chemical Society student chapter at Qatar University was a separate effort, founded in 2015. We built activities, organized scientific discussions, helped students approach instruments, and took chemistry out to audiences who did not owe us their attention. Many people made those events possible. A photograph of me at a festival is a small part of that record.

Years passed. Our specialties changed. The people in the photographs moved on to different work. The scientific conversations did not disappear with the laboratory schedule.

When I return to these pictures, I do not see a single experiment. I see the people who made it natural to have another one in mind.

## Prototype acceptance gate

None of these new versions is licensed to replace its original simply because it has a new color treatment. The pilot sample must pass: **authentic-source attribution, an interesting first 5 seconds, legible faces/data on a phone, editorially meaningful changes of shot, and human listening.** Only then create the full cut and stage it beside the original.

In every case, preserve the full archival image and old MP4 as a separately retrievable record.
