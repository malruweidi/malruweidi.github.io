# Film 13 — What Happens When Immunity Turns on the Gut?
**Editorial narration only, 163-second target. Not yet an audio recording.**

## 00:00–00:12 — A border that is alive
The intestine is more than a passage for food. Every day, its lining must handle a moving world of microorganisms, nutrients, and signals from the body itself.

## 00:12–00:35 — The epithelial barrier
Look closely at the surface. A layer of epithelial cells regulates exchange with the tissue beneath. Their junctions are dynamic. Researchers study how altered barrier function and microbial signals participate in inflammatory disease. But a change in permeability, by itself, is not a diagnosis, or proof of what began an illness.

## 00:35–00:55 — The work of tolerance
Below this border, immune cells continually sample their surroundings. Dendritic cells and lymphocytes help coordinate responses. Much of their work is restraint: identifying a hazard without attacking every harmless food molecule or resident microbe.

## 00:55–01:18 — When signals amplify
Innate receptors detect danger-associated and microbial patterns. Cytokine signals recruit and shape other cells, including adaptive immune populations. When regulation fails, inflammatory responses may become persistent. Yet similar-looking tissue damage can arise through different molecular routes. There is no universal chain of events called 'gut autoimmunity'.

## 01:18–01:52 — Distinct disease biologies
Consider four different examples. Autoimmune gastritis involves immune damage to the stomach's acid-secreting glands. Coeliac disease is a gluten-triggered immune disorder in susceptible individuals. Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis are complex inflammatory bowel diseases. Eosinophilic oesophagitis is a type-two immune-mediated condition and should not be reduced to classical autoimmunity. A common anatomical address does not establish a common pathology.

## 01:52–02:14 — Look closely at evidence
Now the question changes. Which region is affected? What does histology actually show? How do symptoms, exposures, antibodies, and longitudinal findings agree? No schematic can replace a properly interpreted tissue specimen. A theoretical mechanism must remain a hypothesis until specific evidence supports it.

## 02:14–02:31 — The gut–brain connection
The enteric nervous system, vagal pathways, hormones, and immune mediators carry information between the gut and the nervous system. These connections are biologically important. They do not justify reducing chronic intestinal disease to stress or emotion.

## 02:31–02:43 — The central lesson
The 2022 chapter mapped an evolving field. What survives careful reading is the need to keep mechanism, diagnosis, and uncertainty distinct.
