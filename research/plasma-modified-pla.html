Cold plasma, PLA interfaces and the test of a surface

A polymer can retain its bulk identity while its surface chemistry changes the way it wets, adheres or interacts with biological environments. Each property needs its own measurement.

Two related studies, two different endpoints

The Surface and Coatings Technology study investigated electrospun PLA scaffolds modified with ascorbic or fumaric acid after plasma activation, including antimicrobial behaviour. The later Polymers study investigated grafting to PLA films, wettability and adhesion in PLA/aluminium laminates. Scaffold antimicrobial activity must not be confused with the film's peel-resistance result.

From plasma treatment to interpretable surfaces

In the published 2021 PLA-film study, films were plasma-treated in a low-temperature RF setup at 13.56 MHz (reported settings include 80 W, 0.2 Torr and exposure intervals of 30–180 seconds), followed by grafting in ascorbic-acid or fumaric-acid solutions. These are published group methods, not an assertion that I personally operated each instrument at every step.

Complementary characterization included wettability/contact-angle measurement, FTIR and XPS surface chemistry, AFM/SEM topography and peel-resistance measurements. None alone establishes successful grafting and enhanced adhesion: the conclusion rests on convergent chemical, surface and functional measurements and suitable untreated/plasma-only controls.

What the original papers found

The published 2021 film study reports that PLA/aluminium laminate peel resistance increased by 74% after ascorbic-acid modification and by 184% after fumaric-acid modification relative to untreated PLA. These are group findings under specified testing conditions, not new measurements from this website. The 2020 electrospun-scaffold study reports antimicrobial effects against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, measured in a separate experimental system.

Plasma-generated reactivity, surface wettability, adhesive performance, nanomechanics and antimicrobial response are related but not interchangeable endpoints. A material that performs well in an adhesion test is not therefore a clinically validated implant.

My contribution and the published authorship record

I worked experimentally and analytically within Dr. Anton Popelka's collaborative materials programme, and I recall contributing to the development of the scientific manuscript. The formal CRediT statement of the 2021 Polymers paper specifically credits me under investigation and formal analysis, while its original-draft and writing/editing roles are attributed to other listed authors. These two records are distinguished rather than treated as identical claims.

The project gave me direct experience with the difficulty of relating a change in surface preparation to actual measured performance. It also connected my chemistry background to biomaterial interfaces and later therapeutic-delivery questions.
