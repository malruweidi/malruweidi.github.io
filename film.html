A documentary research portrait / 2026

Anatomy of a Question.

Beginning with a school chemistry exhibition in 2009, the film follows experimental failure, living systems, chronic illness, pharmaceutical history and research still in progress. Its central question: what does the evidence allow us to know?

The complete script / English

The narration.

The photograph survives because someone thought to take it. Doha, 2009. Mahmoud stands beside his chemistry teacher, Aref Elahmad, holding a certificate.

An ordinary scene. A student, a teacher, and an early attempt to ask a question about matter.

Two years later, in school anatomy, a fox lies on the table. A textbook had made the organs seem separate.

The real body is less easily divided. Its parts occupy the same space and depend on one another.

Illness was already part of those years. It would remain part of the years ahead: hospital stays, interrupted education, and a daily struggle that no single photograph could capture.

His final examinations suffer. Medical school, the route he wanted, falls away. He graduates and enters chemistry instead.

The seductive version would say illness made him a scientist. But the photograph from 2009 contradicts it.

From 2015 onward, university research becomes shared work. He helps build a student chemistry community alongside his colleagues.

Workshops and festivals make methods public. Other people bring questions, and the equipment does not always cooperate.

An imine reaction looks plausible on paper. A hoped-for ring closure does not follow. Chemistry answers the procedure, not the intention.

A polymer refuses to dissolve. Crosslinking is a hypothesis, not a finding; several causes could produce the same failure.

He calls the notebook A Series of Unfortunate Trials. A joke, perhaps. Still, the conditions of each unsuccessful attempt matter.

Another collaboration gives chemistry a striking colour: curcumin responds to cyanide under defined experimental conditions.

But colour is not a theory. Selectivity, sensitivity and alternative reactions still require measurements and suitable comparisons.

In 2017, Mahmoud buys a small microscope near Qasr El Einy in Cairo. The city recedes. The instrument comes home with him.

On his desk among books and cups, the microscope makes room for questions after the laboratory doors have closed.

Then the scale changes. In developing zebrafish, blood flow, vascular cells and primary cilia interact in time.

The living specimen moves. Video records an event. It cannot, on its own, identify a cause.

During Ramadan, the work continues after iftar. Laboratory hours extend. Prayers are missed, and people wait at home.

One evening, guests are waiting while Mahmoud is still at the microscope. They do not appear in the paper.

The vascular study enters the scientific record. Its figures show methods and observations. The obligations around them remain outside the frame.

In pulmonary research, a moving air-liquid boundary injures cultured epithelial cells. A chamber makes that physical exposure measurable.

A model achieves clarity by simplifying the world. That is its scientific strength, and also its boundary.

An effect in a controlled laboratory system is not automatically a clinical outcome. The living lung is more complicated.

There is no clean division between the researcher and the patient. Illness accompanies his studies, his experiments, and the ordinary work of every day.

Not an episode followed by recovery, and not an interruption confined to a date. A condition he continues to live and work with, across years.

The photographs reveal fragments: fatigue, a pause between examinations. They cannot show every symptom, decision, or effort involved in carrying on with research.

This is not a story of overcoming illness. The research continues alongside it. Neither the work nor the suffering cancels the other.

His historical work opens another kind of laboratory, made of texts. How did pharmacists prepare, substitute and preserve medicines in Cairo?

This Egyptian copy, dated 1904, preserves an older pharmacy text. It is a witness to transmission, not direct proof of medieval practice.

A recipe says what an author recorded. It cannot establish how widely a preparation was used, or by whom.

The historian must compare sources and contexts without pretending that every lost voice can be recovered.

Back in the laboratory, an agarose-and-paraffin histology attempt separates before a useful section can be produced.

The damaged material remains in the record: evidence that this attempt failed, not evidence that its cause was established.

He keeps returning to a practical question: what becomes of a treatment when its physical delivery system changes?

His current master's research investigates inhaled dexamethasone niosomes and proniosomes. Formulation integrity, nebulization and aerosol behaviour still need experimental answers.

It is not a finished medicine. It is work in progress, asking the next experiment to distinguish between alternatives.

Since 2009, the subjects have multiplied. The discipline has not: distinguish what was seen, what may be inferred, and what remains unknown.

The narration uses a licensed synthetic third-person voice. It is not Mahmoud Al-Ruweidi's recorded voice and does not imitate it. The original photographs, archival recordings and scientific figures remain identified by provenance.

Sources and limits

What the film can establish.

Personal chronology is drawn from the original stories and first-person account of illness. The curcumin investigation is documented in Analytical Methods (2019); the zebrafish collaboration in ATVB (2018); and the pulmonary work in Journal of Applied Physiology (2021). These are experiments under specific conditions, not proof of clinical treatment. A historical manuscript shown in the film is a 1904 Egyptian copy of Minhāj al-dukkān from the Wellcome Collection (MS Arabic 1002); its displayed page is not identified as a syrup passage. Current inhaled-formulation research is ongoing.
