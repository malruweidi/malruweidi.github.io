RESEARCH / CHEMISTRY, DELIVERY & BIOLOGICAL SYSTEMS

From molecular design to biological response.

My experimental path began with chemical sensing and synthesis, moved through polymer interfaces and vascular imaging, and reached mechanically injured lung cells and inhalable formulations. Those projects led me to a recurring problem: how do a molecule's structure, a material's preparation and the surrounding biological conditions determine what it actually does?

I want to investigate that relationship at whichever scale best fits the scientific question: molecular recognition, physicochemical behavior and transport, or a measured response in a living model. Kidney disease therapeutics and renal cell-targeted delivery now interest me alongside pulmonary pharmacology and biomaterials. A good PhD project might concentrate on just one of these problems. I am interested in developing deep experimental competence, not assembling a project that contains every technology on this website.

The experimental reason these questions belong together: A chemical sensor needs evidence of selectivity, not merely a changed optical signal. Plasma-treated PLA needs separate evidence of surface modification and functional performance. 01 / PULMONARY CELL INJURY · FIRST AUTHOR

Can altering cell mechanics reduce airway-reopening injury?

Method. Rat L2 epithelial cells were exposed to a propagating air bubble in a parallel-plate chamber. Live/dead fluorescence, actin imaging and atomic-force microscopy provided complementary readouts.

Published finding. Cell death following bubble flow was 54.7 ± 3.94%, compared with 15.1 ± 3.43% after dexamethasone pretreatment. These are study-level measurements under defined in vitro conditions.

My contribution. The journal formally credits my experiments, data analysis, interpretation, figure preparation and original manuscript draft. H. C. Yalcin is credited with conceiving and designing the research.

Boundary. Reduced injury in a rat cell-line model does not establish patient-level treatment efficacy or a uniquely proven protective pathway.

01 / DISCOVERY

Find the state that matters.

Before choosing a drug, determine which molecular interaction or cell state actually drives the phenotype. Protein location, conformation and timing can matter as much as target abundance. Chemical probes, genetic perturbation and functional rescue would test that dependence.

Decisive experiment: does altering the target change the disease-relevant response, rather than binding to it alone?

02 / DELIVERY & MATERIALS

Discover where function is lost.

For a drug or RNA cargo, I would distinguish formulation stability, administered dose, tissue access, cellular uptake and productive intracellular activity. The same logic applies to a surface-modified biomaterial, where chemical treatment must be linked to a measurable function.

Decisive experiment: which step limits effective, tolerable exposure when tested against a matched control?

ADDITIONAL RESEARCH INTEREST / RENAL THERAPEUTICS & DELIVERY

The kidney is not one therapeutic destination.

A glomerular podocyte, an injured proximal-tubule cell and the epithelium lining a renal cyst are different targets, with different access routes and pharmacology. I want to investigate kidney disease therapeutics and the molecular engineering needed to deliver treatment to the relevant renal compartment. The central challenge is to distinguish accumulation in a kidney from entry into the intended cells and, finally, from functional delivery that changes the intended biological response. Glomerular filtration, luminal reabsorption, basolateral access, transporters and disease-related changes in tissue permeability must shape the design, not be treated as afterthoughts.

01 / AKI AND CKD

Can injured tubules be treated without exposing the whole kidney?

In acute kidney injury and progression towards chronic fibrosis, I am interested in injury-conditioned targeting of tubular epithelium. KIM-1/HAVCR1 can serve as an uptake-associated marker in damaged tubules; megalin/cubilin and tubular transporters create different access routes whose activity may change during injury. A 2025 study showed KIM-1-associated delivery of biomimetic HDL nanoparticles carrying triptolide and nintedanib in mouse models of fibrosis. The result supports a targeting hypothesis, not proven human efficacy.

First test: compare free drug, carrier alone, untargeted cargo and targeted cargo, then measure cell-resolved uptake, pharmacodynamic activity, injury and off-organ toxicity. Demonstrate the mechanism of uptake separately from a reduction in fibrosis markers.

He et al., Nature Communications (2025) ↗

02 / GENETIC RENAL DISEASE

Can a therapy reach the cells lining a kidney cyst?

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, especially PKD1/PKD2-associated biology, offers a clear distinction between molecular correction and control of cyst-promoting pathways. A July 2026 preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, reported peptide-functionalized micelles delivering siRNAs against TMEM16A and MCP-1 to collecting duct epithelial cells. Separately, a peer-reviewed mouse study demonstrated AAV-mediated base editing of a disease-associated PKD1 variant. Neither is an approved renal gene therapy.

First test: validate collecting-duct specificity and productive knockdown or restored protein function in matched healthy and cystic renal models; compare cyst growth, inflammatory signals, cargo integrity and systemic distribution.

Giblin et al., bioRxiv preprint (July 2026) ↗ · ADPKD base-editing study, Nature Communications (2025) ↗

03 / GLOMERULAR AND IMMUNE DISEASE

When is renal delivery the right intervention?

IgA nephropathy, complement-mediated glomerular disease and podocyte injury connect immunopharmacology with tissue-selective exposure. A target may reside in the circulating immune system rather than in renal parenchyma: APRIL-directed antibodies and complement factor-B inhibition illustrate why a renal nanocarrier is not automatically preferable. By contrast, a locally acting podocyte or glomerular therapy requires testing its access through the glomerular endothelium and basement membrane.

First test: establish where the causal target acts, then compare systemic pathway modulation with a locally directed intervention using target engagement, proteinuria-relevant functional endpoints and renal safety.

FDA: APRIL-directed therapy in IgA nephropathy (2025) ↗ · Huang et al., renal nanomedicine physiology (2024) ↗

04 / HUMAN RENAL MODELS

Which exposure measurements predict renal function?

Human proximal-tubule-on-chip systems derived from kidney organoids can model polarized transport, including OAT1/3 and OCT2, more faithfully than some conventional immortalized-cell systems. For a renal carrier, I would pair physicochemical characterization with transporter function, directional epithelial transport, intracellular cargo activity and nephrotoxicity. Computational clearance or PBPK models would earn their place through prospective agreement with experimental measurements.

First test: benchmark a transporter-competent chip against a simpler culture using reference substrates and inhibitors, then prospectively compare cellular delivery, barrier integrity and kidney-injury responses.

Ma et al., proximal tubule-on-chip study (2024) ↗

Research approach. I would begin with one specified renal disease and cell population rather than a universal kidney-targeting nanoparticle. Appropriate modalities might include a small molecule, siRNA, a conjugate, an engineered lipid or polymer carrier, or eventually mRNA/circular RNA if the intended biology requires protein production. For RNA-based systems, fluorescence in renal tissue would be a distribution observation; intracellular RNA integrity, target silencing or protein expression, dose-response and renal functional outcomes would determine whether delivery actually worked.

I am particularly interested in molecular pharmaceutics and chemical biology, but an excellent project in synthesis, polymer science, experimental physiology, instrumentation or computational modelling could address just one of these questions. The target, experimental system and methods should be chosen with the laboratory, not declared in advance.

FOUR PROBLEMS / TESTABLE BEGINNINGS

Where I would put the first experiments.

Each proposal begins with a problem that can be tested. The target, model and methods would be refined with the research group, whether the outcome is a clearer mechanism, a new material, a measurement technique or a therapeutic approach.

01 STATE-DEPENDENT TARGETS

Can the vulnerable state be identified before choosing the drug?

Take a signalling interaction involved in pathological repair or tumour invasion. Instead of ranking targets only by abundance, characterize when the relevant protein complex forms, where it resides and what changes after injury or treatment. Compare orthogonal binding measurements with genetic perturbation and functional rescue. A compound that binds convincingly but fails to alter a disease-associated state is not an encouraging hit merely because its affinity is strong. Small-molecule discovery targeting the SLIT2–ROBO axis illustrates how to connect a molecular screening programme to questions of biological function.

First possible test: time-resolved cell states, target engagement, genetic perturbation and rescue. Decisive question: does the target causally control the selected transition?

02 PRODUCTIVE EXPOSURE

Where, exactly, does a promising therapeutic disappear?

In pulmonary RNA therapy a formulation must survive aerosol generation, deposit in the relevant region, interact with lung lining fluid, enter the intended cell and release functional cargo. I would compare delivery systems across those separate barriers in healthy and injury-conditioned models, rather than rank them by size or fluorescent uptake alone. Functional knockdown, intracellular release, barrier recovery and inflammation should be measured alongside delivered dose. Experimental work showing that endosomal escape can provoke inflammatory damage makes the design problem sharper: greater entry need not produce a safer or more effective medicine.

First possible test: matched RNA cargo and administered dose across formulations, measuring aerosol mass balance, cellular delivery, knockdown and tissue function. Decisive question: which barrier limits productive and tolerable exposure?

03 SPATIAL RESPONSE

Why does recognition fail to become treatment?

Antibody–drug conjugates, aptamer conjugates and engineered T cells make recognition central to therapeutic design, but recognition is only the beginning. In an ADC, antigen heterogeneity, internalization, linker cleavage, payload release and bystander toxicity matter. In aptamer-directed carriers the questions include nuclease stability, receptor-dependent uptake and intracellular escape; aptamer-guided oligonucleotide delivery to muscle stem cells is a useful research example. CAR-T and other engineered immune cells confront trafficking, persistence and suppressive microenvironments; tumour-on-chip research on CAR-T action illustrates a possible experimental route. These modalities share questions about access and spatial response, not interchangeable measures of success.

First possible test: one modality and one co-culture or tissue chip, with mapped recognition sites and time-resolved functional response. Decisive question: does failure arise from recognition, access, release or tissue resistance?

04 PROSPECTIVE PREDICTION

Can a model predict the next experiment before seeing it?

I would first model a tractable sequence: formulation characteristics, delivered dose, tissue transport, intracellular disposition and measured pharmacological response. Mechanistic transport, PBPK, QSP or statistical models could each be used where their parameters are identifiable. I would specify predictions before collecting a held-out dataset and assess calibration and systematic error afterward. The model should be able to recommend an informative experiment, and it should also be allowed to fail.

First possible test: a modular inhalation-to-cell-response model linked to aerosol and epithelial-barrier experiments. Decisive question: does it predict unseen results better than a simpler baseline, and where does its uncertainty become unacceptable?

FRONTIERS / A LONGER HORIZON

The field is changing at its boundaries.

I am interested in induced-proximity pharmacology, including targeted degradation and compounds that recruit proteins into functional complexes. Here, measuring binary affinity may be insufficient: ternary-complex geometry, cooperativity and the downstream cellular event become critical. The emerging induced-proximity therapeutic landscape broadens possible targets while making selectivity, cellular access and target-specific functional evidence more important.

Programmable recognition presents another frontier. Aptamers, antibody conjugates, RNA cargoes, logic-gated cellular therapies and responsive materials can make an intervention conditional on its environment. Each architecture creates failure modes of its own, including unstable conjugates, intracellular trapping, antigen escape and on-target injury in normal tissue. In solid tumours, the microenvironment can obstruct engineered immune-cell trafficking and persistence; engineering the therapeutic cell therefore raises questions about the experimental environment used to test it.

I would like to follow the earliest measurable stages of epithelial injury and abnormal repair in longitudinal human-derived models. The experimental design would require controlled mechanical exposure, repeated functional measurements and perturbations capable of distinguishing a reversible response from progressive damage. A human inflammatory-bowel-disease-on-chip investigation demonstrates how disease progression and cancer initiation can be approached experimentally. A measured transition still has to be shown to be causal, not merely correlated with disease.

Finally, the connections between tissue engineering and pharmacometrics are becoming more interesting. Multi-organ tissue-chip research highlights the challenge of preserving the biological niche of each tissue while permitting controlled exchange between them. These systems are not substitutes for human physiology by declaration. Their future value depends on quantitatively linking their readouts to drug transport, model assumptions and independent reference evidence.

A LONG-TERM LABORATORY IDEA

Make the evidence travel with the medicine.

My ideal scientific environment would allow a therapeutic candidate to be interrogated at successive scales without pretending that one researcher should master every instrument. A medicinal-chemistry or chemical-biology question would inform the molecule, conjugate or cell. Formulation and transport experiments would show whether it reaches the intended site in active form. A tissue-engineering experiment would measure what happens in a defined human disease state. Quantitative pharmacology would then ask how far the result can reasonably be extrapolated.

One plausible beginning is pulmonary disease, where my experience with epithelial injury and inhalable formulations provides a concrete entry point. The methods can expand through controlled-release science, cancer chemical biology, engineered immune therapeutics and patient-relevant pharmacometrics. The ambition is portable, but the first biological model must be specific. Over time, I would like to develop open, versioned datasets, reproducible protocols, qualified tissue models and modular mechanistic software. The most important output would be a better decision about which therapeutic hypothesis deserves the next experiment.

Conversations and scientific work that attracted me to different researchers point toward aerosol physics, intracellular RNA delivery, ocular controlled-release systems, molecular recognition, cellular engineering and human variability in drug exposure. They are not the same specialism. I want to learn their distinct reasoning and create experiments in which one discipline can genuinely test another's assumptions.

MOLECULAR RECOGNITION / IMMUNOPHARMACOLOGY

Changing how cells communicate.

Drugs that interfere with protein–protein interactions can change how receptors communicate, but binding alone says little about pathway function. In the work of Moustafa T. Gabr and collaborators, CD28 antagonists inhibit T-cell costimulation, while TREM2 agonists are evaluated through downstream microglial signalling. His group has also identified ROBO2-binding chemical starting points, a direct scientific connection to my own SLIT2–ROBO patent-review interests.

My question: can chemical probes distinguish receptor occupancy from antagonism, agonism and a meaningful cell-state change? I'd connect biophysical assays, cellular engagement, downstream signalling and a phenotype, not just docking scores.

A pharmacological lesson from a different organ: Rasha Alshaikh's work with Katie Ryan and Christian Waeber on siponimod links S1P-receptor biology to endothelial migration, vascular barrier function, in vivo angiogenesis, ocular pharmacokinetics and sustained PLGA delivery. The disease context is retinal, not pulmonary; what transfers is the disciplined chain from receptor action through tissue exposure to functional endpoints. EPIGENETICS / QUANTITATIVE PHARMACOLOGY

What did the drug change inside the cell?

Ted Lakowski's pharmaceutical analysis research at the University of Manitoba is a model of an approach I admire: pairing epigenetic enzyme pharmacology with LC–MS/MS measurements of histone and DNA modifications, drugs and metabolites. HDAC and DOT1L inhibition can alter chromatin chemistry, but a transcriptomic shift should not automatically be read as specific target engagement.

My question: can exposure, enzyme activity, histone-mark changes, downstream gene expression and a cellular phenotype be measured as one causal chain? This would require developing analytical and pharmacological expertise, not simply running an RNA-seq experiment.

PROGRAMMABLE RNA / THERAPEUTIC FUNCTION

Silence a harmful signal, or restore a useful one?

siRNA recruits RNA interference to reduce a transcript; protein-coding circular RNA instead offers a route to sustained expression, provided its translation elements, circularity, purity and delivery work. These are not interchangeable medicines. I am particularly interested in engineering circular-RNA cargo and ionizable lipid carriers while using siRNA as a mechanistically different, hypothesis-dependent comparator, not simply copying an existing pulmonary-siRNA project.

My question: when is gene suppression preferable to functional replacement? In pulmonary vascular biology, BMPR2–SMAD1/5/9–ID1 signalling offers a concrete restorative example: BMPR2 linear mRNA delivery improved experimental rat PAH outcomes; a circular-RNA alternative remains a proposed extension and must be tested separately.

A pulmonary RNA question worth separating: work associated with Katie B. Ryan at UCC tests what happens to siRNA lipoplexes and LNPs during vibrating-mesh nebulisation, from aerosol formation through retained functional silencing. That problem is directly relevant to my experience with inhalable niosomes, but siRNA knockdown and circular-RNA protein expression require different measures of therapeutic success. CHEMICAL ENGINEERING / MODELS / PK–PD

A dose delivered is not a dose that acts.

Microfluidics interests me in two distinct ways: as controlled-mixing technology for reproducible lipid or polymeric carriers, and as a way to create perfused tissue environments where flow, barrier integrity and disease state alter exposure. This connects directly to my work on mechanically injured lung epithelium, and to my current dexamethasone niosome/proniosome thesis. It also opens up systems beyond the lung, including tumour, immune and vascular tissues.

My question: which barrier prevents productive action: carrier destabilization, extracellular transport, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, intracellular pharmacology or tissue-level compensation? An organ-on-chip is useful only if its predicted response can be validated against simpler models and appropriate controls.

Recognition must be measurable: Jing Yong Ye's UTSA group has reported modified-aptamer functionalization of photonic-crystal biosensors, and co-developed a liver-on-chip platform for label-free monitoring of drug-induced toxicity. For me, the transferable engineering questions are binding specificity, aptamer conformation and stability, signal calibration, and how an affinity readout differs from productive drug delivery. 07 / EXAMPLE STUDIES

How questions become testable experiments.

The next two concepts are worked examples of designing a hypothesis and testing it. They demonstrate how I think, not the only projects I would accept. In a chemistry, biomaterials, biology, pharmaceutics, computational or engineering laboratory, I would develop a project around the laboratory’s expertise and a question that can be answered with available methods. PROTAC synthesis, circular-RNA engineering and organ-on-chip construction remain future training goals, not skills I already claim to possess.

Target engagement through chemical design.

Choose one disease-relevant protein state and test whether a carefully designed intervention changes its function. I want to learn and use bioorthogonal click chemistry and bioconjugation, bifunctional degraders such as PROTACs, molecular glues and aptamer-based recognition where the biology justifies each tool. Pair molecular characterization with target engagement, degradation or functional rescue, rather than relying on binding alone.

Delivery as a pharmacological experiment.

Choose a justified therapeutic cargo, distinguishing siRNA-mediated silencing from protein-producing circular RNA, alongside aptamer conjugates or targeted small molecules, and determine where productive exposure is lost. Develop lipid/polymeric or other appropriate carriers, investigate microfluidic formulation and transport, and measure intracellular fate and functional dose response in relevant cellular models. Connect formulation engineering to PK/PD and mechanistic predictions.

Ideas I learn from across fields.

These selected publications illustrate scientific approaches I admire, not a list of laboratories I will apply to, required future supervisors or research programmes I insist on copying. I read across disciplines because their methods illuminate the problems in my own experimental work.

Moustafa T. Gabr connects medicinal chemistry, target discovery and functional validation in difficult receptor systems, including CD28, TREM2 and the SLIT–ROBO family. A researcher in development, not a fixed specialty.

What I bring now: documented experimental chemistry and materials research, cell-based physiological experiments, microscopy and imaging-system implementation, formulation development, quantitative interpretation, scientific writing and collaborative computational research. Each project has its own author-contribution boundaries; the record and source papers document those differences.

Where I could grow: organic and medicinal synthesis; chemical biology and advanced analytical methods; biochemistry and molecular pharmacology; drug formulation, transport and PK/PD; polymer and biomaterial engineering; cell and tissue models; microfluidic platforms; and computational or imaging methods. Specialized interests such as aptamers, siRNA, circular RNA, PROTACs and targeted conjugates may be relevant when a project calls for them. I do not expect one PhD to teach or deploy all these technologies.

I value careful experimental reasoning regardless of whether the immediate output is a new molecule, an instrument, a biomaterial, a biological mechanism, a dosage form or a therapeutic candidate. For translational projects, I am particularly interested in connecting chemical design to exposure and functional response. For fundamental projects, a strong mechanistic result is worthwhile in its own right.
