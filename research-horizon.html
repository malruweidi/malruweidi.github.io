RESEARCH ESSAY / EXPERIMENTS AND QUESTIONS

Where I want the research to go.

I want to know how a therapeutic intervention reaches diseased cells, engages its target and changes their behaviour, rather than merely looking promising in an early experiment.

I kept trying to dissolve a copolymer that refused to behave as expected. In lung-cell experiments, I studied injury caused by a moving air–liquid interface. My current MSc work asks what happens to a drug carrier when it has to be nebulized. Those experiments pushed me towards three connected scientific questions: what molecular design can control, how materials and formulations behave under real conditions, and how to determine whether a biological response is genuine and mechanistically interpretable. I am especially interested in molecular pharmaceutics and chemical biology, while recognizing that a strong doctoral project may investigate only one part of that relationship.

The central question I want to investigate is how a therapeutic intervention can be designed to act selectively in diseased tissues and produce a measurable response. The work I have already done on inhalable vesicles and epithelial injury gives me a practical starting point: stability during administration is not the same as cell exposure, and cell exposure is not the same as pharmacological effect. Targeted biologics, RNA treatments, engineered cells and other platforms could help answer different parts of that question. I would select one tractable problem with the host laboratory, rather than insist on all those modalities.

THE PROBLEM / 00

What does a promising result actually predict?

A promising binding measurement often fails to predict tissue exposure or cellular activity. Nanocarriers can change during administration, and culture conditions can determine whether an apparent biological response is reproducible. I want to examine these transitions experimentally, measuring delivery and target engagement under the conditions in which a therapy is intended to work.

There is no single modality I regard as the answer. Small molecules, targeted protein degraders such as PROTACs, molecular glues, RNA medicines, aptamer-directed systems, antibody–drug conjugates and engineered immune cells pose different design problems. I am particularly interested in how click chemistry and bioorthogonal bioconjugation can connect recognition elements, linkers and therapeutic cargo where those reactions offer a genuine functional advantage. What interests me is how the target, the intervention and its route through a living system constrain one another. The underlying questions should survive a change of molecule, carrier or disease model.

THE INTELLECTUAL METHOD / 00A

What each discipline cannot see alone.

I first approached science through molecules: what could be made, measured and inferred from structure. Materials introduced a different problem, because composition alone did not determine performance at an interface. Studying vascular development and mechanically injured epithelial cells brought me into systems where geometry, forces, timing and cellular state altered the meaning of a molecular result. Formulation made the next gap unavoidable: a drug can be chemically plausible and still fail on the journey to its site of action.

I want each research question to specify what would count as evidence before the experiment begins. For a binding hypothesis, that might require orthogonal measurements and a functional rescue experiment; for a delivery system, intracellular release and a dose–response relationship; for a computational prediction, an external or prospective test. The aim is to distinguish a plausible mechanism from one the measurements can actually support.

My motivation is to connect sound molecular and material science to a convincing experimental explanation, whether the work focuses on a synthetic method, a formulation, an engineered measurement or a cellular mechanism. These questions can be investigated separately, with depth appropriate to the host laboratory. I want to work with scientists who can explain what their experiments prove, and just as importantly what remains uncertain.

FUTURE INVESTIGATIONS / 01—04

Questions I want to work on.

The common problem is the relationship between therapeutic design, the exposure achieved in living tissue and the biological response that follows. Each investigation below isolates a different uncertainty. A doctoral project could address one through chemistry, pharmaceutics, materials engineering or experimental biology; none requires the full collection of techniques.

01 DISCOVERY

Finding the biological state worth changing.

I am interested in target identification that moves beyond a static protein name or an expression measurement. A disease-associated state may be defined by conformation, interacting partners, location within a cell, trafficking behaviour or its timing during disease progression. This leads naturally to chemical biology, allosteric modulators, induced-proximity pharmacology, PROTACs and molecular-glue degraders, and phenotypic discovery. I want to learn how click chemistry and bioorthogonal ligation can be used to construct or interrogate degraders, probes and ligand conjugates, while separately testing binding, target engagement, degradation selectivity and functional rescue. The important experiment is the one that connects target engagement to a change in disease-relevant function.

Possible tools for doctoral training: medicinal and click chemistry, structure–activity relationships, chemical probes, target-engagement assays, proteomic selectivity measurements, genetic perturbation, live-cell measurements and mechanistic modelling.

02 THERAPEUTIC ENGINEERING

Designing the intervention and its route together.

I want to investigate molecular recognition alongside the chemistry that determines tissue access and intracellular delivery. In nucleic-acid formulations that means measuring uptake and endosomal release as well as functional activity; in antibody–drug conjugates it means examining antigen recognition together with linker and payload behaviour. Aptamer conjugates, lipid and polymeric nanocarriers, responsive biomaterials and engineered cell therapies raise related questions, although their experimental endpoints differ substantially. I am interested in aptamer selection and stabilization, site-selective conjugation, RNA and small-molecule cargo, controlled release, and microfluidic preparation of delivery systems when it enables reproducible manufacturing or precise formulation control.

Therapeutic engineering can also mean modifying a cell or its immediate environment. Cell-surface-bound drug depots, engineered macrophages and other immune cells, and controlled cytokine release offer ways to steer inflammatory or tissue-repair responses. For example, cellular backpacks for macrophage immunotherapy and backpack-assisted myeloid cells in a preclinical multiple-sclerosis model illustrate how particle engineering and cell-state control can be studied together. I am interested in the underlying methods of cell manipulation, signalling measurement and local therapeutic action, not in requiring a particular carrier design.

Possible tools for doctoral training: bioorthogonal bioconjugation, nucleic-acid chemistry, microfluidic formulation, nanocarrier characterization, uptake and endosomal-release assays, receptor trafficking, cellular pharmacology, quantitative PK/PD and biocompatibility tests.

03 HUMAN BIOLOGY

Building models of disease before its late manifestations.

I am particularly interested in how a healthy tissue becomes susceptible to disease and how that transition might be studied before an irreversible endpoint is reached. Patient-derived organoids, co-cultures, tissue engineering and microfluidic organs-on-chips can introduce interactions absent from conventional two-dimensional assays. Engineering methods such as controlled perfusion, shear stress, barrier transport and tissue-interface measurements can help explain why a molecularly promising therapy fails in a more realistic biological environment. In pulmonary or renal models, flow, mechanical loading, barrier transport and inflammatory signalling can be measured under controlled conditions. In oncology, tumour organoids and engineered immune microenvironments could help investigate heterogeneous response and therapeutic resistance.

I want such models to reduce animal use where they can answer the question reliably, and eventually replace particular animal experiments when fit-for-purpose evidence justifies that substitution. An organoid is not automatically more predictive because it is three-dimensional; maturity, vascular or immune components, reproducibility and clinically relevant exposure must be demonstrated.

Possible tools: microphysiological systems, organoids, 3D bioprinting, longitudinal imaging, tissue function assays and systematic model qualification.

04 PREDICTIVE SYSTEMS

Making experiments and computational models correct one another.

I want to develop computational tools that follow more of a therapeutic candidate's life: from molecular properties and formulation through administration, transport, metabolism, target exposure and biological response. Molecular simulation, in-silico screening, physiologically based pharmacokinetics, quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning can all contribute, but they answer different questions and carry different assumptions. The objective would be a testable model linked to measurements, with its scope and uncertainty explicit.

A valuable result would be a workflow in which a model identifies the next informative experiment, the experimental result updates the model, and uncertainty is reported alongside predictions. I am interested in this logic of design–make–test–analyse, including automation where the underlying measurements justify it.

Possible tools: scientific Python, molecular descriptors, PBPK/QSP, mechanistic simulation, imaging analytics, uncertainty analysis and human-in-the-loop closed-loop experimentation.

FUTURE DIRECTION / DISCOVERY TO PRODUCTION

Could discovery and synthesis form one experimental loop?

One research direction I would like to pursue is a connected workflow that begins with a disease-relevant cellular phenotype and ends with experimentally validated molecules whose production routes can also be improved. It is an ambitious programme to build collaboratively, not a list of techniques I have already performed.

01 DISCOVER

Which targets and candidate molecules are worth pursuing?

Integrate cellular phenotypes, genetic perturbations, pathway evidence and, where appropriate, transcriptomic or proteomic data to prioritize targets. Identify known ligands and candidate natural-product or synthetic scaffolds. Docking and molecular dynamics can inform hypotheses about recognition; biochemical target engagement, cellular assays and appropriate counter-screens are needed to test them.

02 MAKE

Which chemical or biological route is feasible?

Trace candidate molecules to purchasable intermediates, natural precursors or metabolites. Investigate the enzymes and host organisms that can generate relevant intermediates, including pathway engineering and enzyme promiscuity where supported by evidence. Compare computer-assisted retrosynthetic routes with biocatalytic, chemoenzymatic and continuous-flow methods; measure actual yield, selectivity and process robustness.

03 LEARN

Can screening guide the next synthesis rather than merely report a hit?

Use well-controlled automated assays to quantify dose response, target specificity and cellular phenotype. Feed those data, alongside reaction performance and analytical quality, into experimental design or active-learning models to choose the next set of compounds and conditions. A useful success criterion is prospective improvement confirmed in held-out experiments, not a higher predicted docking score.

Potential methods for future training: target deconvolution and chemical proteomics; structure-based screening; enzyme and metabolic-pathway mining; retrosynthesis planning; reaction and flow engineering; high-throughput assay development; design of experiments and Bayesian optimization.

Background reading: Ha et al., Cell Chemical Biology (2021), experimental approaches to target identification; Kataria et al., RSC Medicinal Chemistry (2024), computational approaches to target and lead identification. These reviews inform the proposed methodology; they do not constitute results from this proposed programme.

FOUR PROBLEMS / TESTABLE BEGINNINGS

Where I would put the first experiments.

Each proposal begins with a problem that can be tested. The target, model and methods would be refined with the research group, whether the outcome is a clearer mechanism, a new material, a measurement technique or a therapeutic approach.

01 STATE-DEPENDENT TARGETS

Can the vulnerable state be identified before choosing the drug?

Take a signalling interaction involved in pathological repair or tumour invasion. Instead of ranking targets only by abundance, characterize when the relevant protein complex forms, where it resides and what changes after injury or treatment. Compare orthogonal binding measurements with genetic perturbation and functional rescue. A compound that binds convincingly but fails to alter a disease-associated state is not an encouraging hit merely because its affinity is strong. Small-molecule discovery targeting the SLIT2–ROBO axis illustrates how to connect a molecular screening programme to questions of biological function.

First possible test: time-resolved cell states, target engagement, genetic perturbation and rescue. Decisive question: does the target causally control the selected transition?

02 PRODUCTIVE EXPOSURE

Where, exactly, does a promising therapeutic disappear?

In pulmonary RNA therapy a formulation must survive aerosol generation, deposit in the relevant region, interact with lung lining fluid, enter the intended cell and release functional cargo. I would compare delivery systems across those separate barriers in healthy and injury-conditioned models, rather than rank them by size or fluorescent uptake alone. Functional knockdown, intracellular release, barrier recovery and inflammation should be measured alongside delivered dose. Experimental work showing that endosomal escape can provoke inflammatory damage makes the design problem sharper: greater entry need not produce a safer or more effective medicine.

First possible test: matched RNA cargo and administered dose across formulations, measuring aerosol mass balance, cellular delivery, knockdown and tissue function. Decisive question: which barrier limits productive and tolerable exposure?

03 SPATIAL RESPONSE

Why does recognition fail to become treatment?

Antibody–drug conjugates, aptamer conjugates and engineered T cells make recognition central to therapeutic design, but recognition is only the beginning. In an ADC, antigen heterogeneity, internalization, linker cleavage, payload release and bystander toxicity matter. In aptamer-directed carriers the questions include nuclease stability, receptor-dependent uptake and intracellular escape; aptamer-guided oligonucleotide delivery to muscle stem cells is a useful research example. CAR-T and other engineered immune cells confront trafficking, persistence and suppressive microenvironments; tumour-on-chip research on CAR-T action illustrates a possible experimental route. These modalities share questions about access and spatial response, not interchangeable measures of success.

First possible test: one modality and one co-culture or tissue chip, with mapped recognition sites and time-resolved functional response. Decisive question: does failure arise from recognition, access, release or tissue resistance?

04 PROSPECTIVE PREDICTION

Can a model predict the next experiment before seeing it?

I would first model a tractable sequence: formulation characteristics, delivered dose, tissue transport, intracellular disposition and measured pharmacological response. Mechanistic transport, PBPK, QSP or statistical models could each be used where their parameters are identifiable. I would specify predictions before collecting a held-out dataset and assess calibration and systematic error afterward. The model should be able to recommend an informative experiment, and it should also be allowed to fail.

First possible test: a modular inhalation-to-cell-response model linked to aerosol and epithelial-barrier experiments. Decisive question: does it predict unseen results better than a simpler baseline, and where does its uncertainty become unacceptable?

FRONTIERS / A LONGER HORIZON

The field is changing at its boundaries.

I am interested in induced-proximity pharmacology, including targeted degradation and compounds that recruit proteins into functional complexes. Here, measuring binary affinity may be insufficient: ternary-complex geometry, cooperativity and the downstream cellular event become critical. The emerging induced-proximity therapeutic landscape broadens possible targets while making selectivity, cellular access and target-specific functional evidence more important.

Programmable recognition presents another frontier. Aptamers, antibody conjugates, RNA cargoes, logic-gated cellular therapies and responsive materials can make an intervention conditional on its environment. Each architecture creates failure modes of its own, including unstable conjugates, intracellular trapping, antigen escape and on-target injury in normal tissue. In solid tumours, the microenvironment can obstruct engineered immune-cell trafficking and persistence; engineering the therapeutic cell therefore raises questions about the experimental environment used to test it.

I would like to follow the earliest measurable stages of epithelial injury and abnormal repair in longitudinal human-derived models. The experimental design would require controlled mechanical exposure, repeated functional measurements and perturbations capable of distinguishing a reversible response from progressive damage. A human inflammatory-bowel-disease-on-chip investigation demonstrates how disease progression and cancer initiation can be approached experimentally. A measured transition still has to be shown to be causal, not merely correlated with disease.

Finally, the connections between tissue engineering and pharmacometrics are becoming more interesting. Multi-organ tissue-chip research highlights the challenge of preserving the biological niche of each tissue while permitting controlled exchange between them. These systems are not substitutes for human physiology by declaration. Their future value depends on quantitatively linking their readouts to drug transport, model assumptions and independent reference evidence.

CURRENT INVESTIGATION / 03

Where I am working now.

My ongoing MSc thesis investigates inhalable dexamethasone-loaded niosomes and proniosomes for pulmonary delivery. The immediate pharmaceutical questions involve vesicular composition, characterization and the behaviour of the system during nebulization. My documented development work includes formulation calculations and a Python niosome-design tool. Assay development, particle measurements and aerodynamic deposition are separate experimental endpoints, whose status and limitations I record in the project dossier.

This project has made one set of translational constraints tangible, while my research interests extend across therapeutic discovery and experimental bioengineering. It has made the separation between a formulation's measured properties and its intended biological exposure particularly concrete. The long-term work I want to pursue will require learning new experimental systems and contributing with collaborators whose expertise differs from my own.

A LONG-TERM LABORATORY IDEA

Make the evidence travel with the medicine.

My ideal scientific environment would allow a therapeutic candidate to be interrogated at successive scales without pretending that one researcher should master every instrument. A medicinal-chemistry or chemical-biology question would inform the molecule, conjugate or cell. Formulation and transport experiments would show whether it reaches the intended site in active form. A tissue-engineering experiment would measure what happens in a defined human disease state. Quantitative pharmacology would then ask how far the result can reasonably be extrapolated.

One plausible beginning is pulmonary disease, where my experience with epithelial injury and inhalable formulations provides a concrete entry point. The methods can expand through controlled-release science, cancer chemical biology, engineered immune therapeutics and patient-relevant pharmacometrics. The ambition is portable, but the first biological model must be specific. Over time, I would like to develop open, versioned datasets, reproducible protocols, qualified tissue models and modular mechanistic software. The most important output would be a better decision about which therapeutic hypothesis deserves the next experiment.

Conversations and scientific work that attracted me to different researchers point toward aerosol physics, intracellular RNA delivery, ocular controlled-release systems, molecular recognition, cellular engineering and human variability in drug exposure. They are not the same specialism. I want to learn their distinct reasoning and create experiments in which one discipline can genuinely test another's assumptions.

EVIDENCE AND LIMITS / 05

What would make the model useful?

Before using an organoid or computational model to rank candidate therapies, I would define its context of use and compare its performance against an appropriate reference. Maturity of the biological model, reproducibility across preparations and prospective predictive accuracy would determine whether additional complexity is useful.

Work on human tissue chips and new approach methodologies provides a serious basis for reducing animal experimentation in suitable applications, but replacement cannot be asserted universally. My ambition is to develop and evaluate models that are informative for the biological decision at hand, and to change the model when the evidence requires it.
