RUWAIDIX / AN EVOLVING RESEARCH DIRECTION

Where I want the research to go.

I want to work on medicines, and on the methods by which we decide whether a medicine has a serious chance of working.

My background runs from synthesis and molecular sensing through materials, developing vessels and mechanically injured lung cells to pharmaceutical formulations. I have learned how much changes when a molecular claim is tested in a biological system. The next questions I want to investigate concern target choice, therapeutic design, delivery and the ability of experimental models to predict what happens in human tissue.

THE PROBLEM / 00

What does a promising result actually predict?

A strong binding assay can identify a molecular interaction without telling us whether a drug will reach the target in tissue. A delivery vehicle can remain stable in a vial but change during administration. An apparently convincing cell response may depend on a culture condition that does not exist in disease. I want to study these discontinuities as engineering and pharmacological problems, not treat them as somebody else's work at the next stage of development.

There is no single modality I regard as the answer. Small molecules, targeted protein degraders, RNA medicines, aptamer-directed systems, antibody–drug conjugates and engineered immune cells pose different design problems. What interests me is how the target, the intervention and its route through a living system constrain one another. The underlying questions should survive a change of molecule, carrier or disease model.

FUTURE INVESTIGATIONS / 01—04

Questions I want to work on.

These questions connect across disciplines, although each investigation would need a narrower hypothesis and an experimental system suited to it.

01 DISCOVERY

Finding the biological state worth changing.

I am interested in target identification that moves beyond a static protein name or an expression measurement. A disease-associated state may be defined by conformation, interacting partners, location within a cell, trafficking behaviour or its timing during disease progression. This leads naturally to chemical biology, allosteric modulators, induced-proximity pharmacology, targeted degradation and phenotypic discovery. The important experiment is the one that connects target engagement to a change in disease-relevant function.

Possible tools: molecular and structural analysis, perturbation screens, live-cell measurements, mechanistic modelling and chemical probes.

02 THERAPEUTIC ENGINEERING

Designing the intervention and its route together.

Drug discovery cannot be separated neatly from delivery when activity depends on tissue access, cellular uptake, endosomal escape, intracellular release or persistence. I want to explore how carrier chemistry and the biology of recognition can be co-designed: nucleic-acid delivery, aptamers and their conjugates, antibody–drug conjugate linkers and payloads, responsive biomaterials, and engineered cell therapies including CAR-T systems. Different modalities require different assays. Their common challenge is to produce an effect at the relevant site without assuming that target binding guarantees useful exposure.

Possible tools: medicinal chemistry, bioconjugation, formulation, biointerfaces, receptor-trafficking assays, cellular engineering and quantitative PK/PD.

03 HUMAN BIOLOGY

Building models of disease before its late manifestations.

I am particularly interested in how a healthy tissue becomes susceptible to disease and how that transition might be studied before an irreversible endpoint is reached. Patient-derived organoids, co-cultures, tissue engineering and organs-on-chips can introduce interactions absent from conventional two-dimensional assays. In pulmonary or renal models, flow, mechanical loading, barrier transport and inflammatory signalling can be measured under controlled conditions. In oncology, tumour organoids and engineered immune microenvironments could help investigate heterogeneous response and therapeutic resistance.

I want such models to reduce animal use where they can answer the question reliably, and eventually replace particular animal experiments when fit-for-purpose evidence justifies that substitution. An organoid is not automatically more predictive because it is three-dimensional; maturity, vascular or immune components, reproducibility and clinically relevant exposure must be demonstrated.

Possible tools: microphysiological systems, organoids, 3D bioprinting, longitudinal imaging, tissue function assays and systematic model qualification.

04 PREDICTIVE SYSTEMS

Making experiments and computational models correct one another.

I want to develop computational tools that follow more of a therapeutic candidate's life: from molecular properties and formulation through administration, transport, metabolism, target exposure and biological response. Molecular simulation, in-silico screening, physiologically based pharmacokinetics, quantitative systems pharmacology and machine learning can all contribute, but they answer different questions and carry different assumptions. The objective would be a testable model linked to measurements, with its scope and uncertainty explicit.

A valuable result would be a workflow in which a model identifies the next informative experiment, the experimental result updates the model, and uncertainty is reported alongside predictions. I am interested in this logic of design–make–test–analyse, including automation where the underlying measurements justify it.

Possible tools: scientific Python, molecular descriptors, PBPK/QSP, mechanistic simulation, imaging analytics, uncertainty analysis and human-in-the-loop closed-loop experimentation.

CURRENT INVESTIGATION / 03

Where I am working now.

My MSc research with Prof. Abdelbary Elhissi concerns dexamethasone-loaded niosomes and proniosomes for inhalation. The immediate pharmaceutical questions involve vesicular composition, characterization and the behaviour of the system during nebulization. My documented development work includes formulation calculations and a Python niosome-design tool. Assay development, particle measurements and aerodynamic deposition are separate experimental endpoints, whose status and limitations I record in the project dossier.

This project has made one set of translational constraints tangible, while my research interests extend across therapeutic discovery and experimental bioengineering. It has made the separation between a formulation's measured properties and its intended biological exposure particularly concrete. The long-term work I want to pursue will require learning new experimental systems and contributing with collaborators whose expertise differs from my own.

EVIDENCE AND LIMITS / 05

What would make the model useful?

A future platform should earn confidence through a specified context of use, reproducible measurements and comparisons with suitable reference evidence. A human-derived organoid can fail to model mature tissue. A mechanistic simulation can fit observed data yet fail prospectively. Adding more cellular components or computational complexity does not guarantee a better decision.

Work on human tissue chips and new approach methodologies provides a serious basis for reducing animal experimentation in suitable applications, but replacement cannot be asserted universally. My ambition is to develop and evaluate models that are informative for the biological decision at hand, and to change the model when the evidence requires it.
