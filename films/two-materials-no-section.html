01 / The angle matters

Under a microscope, a zebrafish embryo can look remarkably clear. Cutting a useful series of sections is another matter. A small turn can change which anatomy a blade will reach.

Source: Actual owner-supplied specimen photograph; image does not depict histological sections.
02 / The orientation problem

I wanted to hold each specimen at the right angle before embedding it for histology. Two-percent agarose offered a way to keep that orientation fixed.

Source: Illustrative diagram of 2% agarose pre-embedding. Not actual footage.
03 / Before the blade

After processing, paraffin was supposed to support the block through the microtome. The workflow looked reasonable because each material solved a different practical problem.

Source: Photograph from the actual attempted histology preparation.
04 / The section that never formed

But when the blade met the block, it did not produce the ribbon I needed. Two layers separated, and fragments collected where interpretable sections should have been.

Source: Actual owner-supplied image of an unsuccessful paraffin/agarose attempt; no usable section resulted.
05 / Not a machine failure

The problem was not a dramatic malfunction of the microtome. What failed was the material assembly that the machine had been given to cut.

Source: Original image documents the visibly separated specimen block.
06 / A question of materials

Agarose is water-loving; paraffin is not. Their different behavior offers a plausible explanation for the interface, but my photographs alone cannot prove the exact failure mechanism.

Source: Material-interface explanation is a hypothesis, not an established causal analysis.
07 / No corrected protocol claimed

Changing the agarose grade, processing conditions, or embedding strategy might have answered it. I did not establish which correction would work, and I will not call the attempt successful.

Source: Alternatives are untested possibilities, not recommendations or successful results.
08 / The experimental record

I kept the photographs because they describe a real outcome: the specimen never became a usable section. An experiment can end before the measurement it was designed to produce.

Source: Original failed attempt, unaltered.

03 / no invented explanation

What the record can establish.

The illustration of a mechanism must be answerable to the experiment, not the other way around.

There was an orientation problem, an attempted materials solution, and a visible failure during sectioning. The study did not produce reliable sections for interpreting zebrafish anatomy.

What was observed?

The 2% agarose support did not integrate into a mechanically coherent paraffin block under the attempted processing conditions. Separation and fragmentation interfered with microtomy. No interpretable histological section was obtained.

What explanation was considered?

The distinct hydrophilicity and processing behaviour of agarose and paraffin suggest a materials-interface problem. Agarose grade and thermal behaviour were among the suspected contributors. That possibility was not established as the sole cause; no follow-up experiment should be retroactively invented.

What could a future protocol investigate?

A properly documented comparison could examine embedding strategy, agarose concentration or grade, dehydration and clearing conditions, and the mechanical integrity of the final block. These are proposals for method development, not activities claimed to have been completed.

Source distinction: The three photographs document stages and objects associated with the attempt. The narrative is a first-person experimental recollection. The materials explanation is an interpretation with stated uncertainty; it is not a new publication or an authenticated protocol validation.
