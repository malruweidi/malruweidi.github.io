## 1. When the Count Could Not Describe the Pain (`pub-essay-life-between-diagnoses`)

**Problem found in current screenplay:** Scenes 1 and 2 both begin by saying the speaker finished school at sixteen, and both explain the lost medical-school path. Repetition before the medical narrative starts consumes 33 seconds of a two-minute movie.

- **Scene 1 / 16 seconds / archival image.** Revised narration: “I finished high school at sixteen. Hospital admissions had interrupted much of my final year, and my examination results cost me the medical-school path I had been preparing for.” Keep this as a photographically truthful archival opening, not an invented classroom reenactment.
- **Scene 2 / 17 seconds / childhood archive.** Revised narration: “Chemistry came next. I learned to observe materials under a microscope and ask questions that experiments could answer. The illness did not wait for a convenient time to return.” Transition away from a second graduation explanation. No undated photograph should be captioned as the date of the examinations.
- **Scene 4 / 16 seconds / documented firsthand illness narrative.** Revised narration: “A week before I was due to travel to the United States, another attack led to an endoscopy and a course of glucocorticoids. I was already ill when the treatment began.” This makes the actual flare the active event without defensive on-screen phrasing. Do not represent the photo as the actual endoscopy.

## 2. The Microscope That Made Curiosity Independent (`pub-cairo-microscope`)

**Problem found:** The purchase near Qasr El Einy is independently narrated in scenes 1 and 2, and a Cairo skyline substitutes for the microscope twice. The film needs the physical instrument as its main character.

- **Scene 1 / 17 seconds / Cairo to instrument reveal.** Revised narration: “Near Qasr El Einy in Cairo, I bought a microscope for myself. Getting it home to Qatar became another problem altogether, and I was determined to bring it anyway.” Use authentic Cairo location context with an immediate cut to the actual owner's microscope, not staged transport footage.
- **Scene 2 / 16 seconds / actual microscope portrait.** Revised narration: “The instrument was mine. I could examine a slide without a laboratory booking, return to it another morning, and follow questions that had not yet become formal research projects.” Slow optical push on the eyepiece and focus mechanism from the real owner's photographic evidence. Don't imply newly filmed 2017 material.
- **Visual remedy:** Produce visually distinct macro moves from the documented microscope itself, slide-stage and eyepiece; alternate with real personal observations. Avoid substituting generic stock laboratory videos as original home microscopy.

## 3. A Breath Can Injure (`pub-airway-reopening`)

**Problem found:** Scene 2 speaks about a machine keeping *a patient* breathing, then uses cultured rat alveolar epithelial monolayers as evidence. This narrative jump risks implying the study directly measured clinical ventilator injury.

- **Scene 2 / 14 seconds / real flow-chamber research figure.** Revised narration: “The moving air–liquid boundary can impose forces on epithelial cells. We used an experimental flow chamber to study one such event under controlled conditions.” Focus on the experimental geometry; do not present in-vitro results as direct measurements of patient injury.
- **Scene 3 / 15 seconds / original motion schematic.** Keep the existing precise “single air bubble passing over a monolayer” description and mark the animation clearly **schematic, not microscopy**. Preserve actual reported in-vitro measurements as given in the cited paper, DOI 10.1152/japplphysiol.00574.2020.

## 4. How Did Medieval Medicines Keep? (`pub-making-medicines-keep`)

**Problem found:** Scenes 6 and 7 repeat “reading against the temptation to romanticise”, and both spend their runtime repeating that historical sources do not prove clinical outcomes. Their distinct evidence functions are buried.

- **Scene 6 / 15 seconds / a clearly identified documentary witness.** Revised narration: “A prescription from the Cairo Genizah is a different kind of source from a formulary. Its surviving words can show a particular documentary practice, without describing every pharmacy in Cairo.” Confirm the specific document, date and archival rights before inserting a photographed manuscript; otherwise use typographic quotation from verified transcribed text with direct source credit.
- **Scene 7 / 16 seconds / methods of historical inference.** Revised narration: “The historical question is how makers judged concentration, consistency and suitable substitution. Those are decisions visible in instructions, even when the preparation itself no longer survives for chemical testing.” Use process-oriented visual treatment without claiming a validated medieval shelf-life test.

Phonetic transliteration must be checked by an Arabic reader; never trust unreviewed English TTS to infer historical Arabic pronunciation. ## Directing rule for all 44

Current source audit: 44 titles, 367 shots, approximately 6,943 narration words; 40 films at exactly 120 seconds, 32 with no embedded video shot, and research imagery recurring 8–14 times in different works. These figures describe the **source storyboard**, not whether its finished frames contain camera movement. Fix repetitive source selection before buying or generating more imagery. Film length should respond to the argument and archive rather than being automatically fixed at 120 seconds.

Scientific films: start with an observable problem, situate a physical or chemical mechanism, examine the actual material and result, and disclose uncertainty where it arises. Unsuccessful studies must retain failed outcomes. Personal films: begin with a physical object or documented experience rather than generic personal-brand claims. Historical films: distinguish authentic manuscript/object evidence from stylized reconstruction in-frame, identify geographical and chronological limits, and keep textual claims traceable. Narrative voice should sound written for this film only.
